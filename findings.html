06 - The Data

Key Findings

The side-by-side results of male and female responses reveal striking differences in how each gender perceives comfort, credibility, and assumptions in sports spaces.

01

Comfort as a Sports Fan in Front of Women

The participants were asked: "How comfortable do you feel identifying as a sports fan in front of women?" The results show general comfort from both groups, but with a notable caveat from men. 25% of men reported feeling neutral rather than fully comfortable. Women showed even higher confidence in this setting, with 83.3% feeling very comfortable around other women.

Key Takeaway

Both men and women feel broadly comfortable identifying as a sports fan in front of women. This suggests female sports converasations carry relatively low social pressure around identity regardless of gender.

02

Comfort as a Sports Fan in Front of Men

The participants were asked: "How comfortable do you feel identifying as a sports fan in front of men?" The women's responses were spread across four categories with only 16.7% feeling very comfortable. The men, on the other hand, split more evenly, with half feeling very comfortable and the rest divided between somewhat comfortable and neutral. The data suggests women experience notably more hesitation in male-dominated sports spaces.

Key Takeaway

The presence of men dramatically reduces women's comfort level as self-identified sports fans. This aligns with the hypothesis that male-dominated environments create real barriers to women's participation in sports conversations.

03

Questioned Sports Knowledge Because of Gender

The participants were asked: "Have you ever had your sports knowledge questioned because of your gender?" Two-thirds of women reported that their sports knowledge has been questioned because of their gender, while 33.3% were unsure. Not a single woman said "no." Men showed the opposite: 100% said their sports knowledge has never been questioned on the basis of gender. This data reveals a deeply unequal experience between the two groups.

04

Assumptions of Male Sports Fans

The participants were asked: "Which of the following best describes your first assumption when a man identifies as a sports fan?" When a man says he's a sports fan, both women and men overwhelmingly assume he genuinely enjoys the sport. Women assumed this at a rate of 66.7%, with the remaining 33.3% believing he follows it casually. Men showed even more automatic faith in each other with 75% assumed genuine enjoyment, with only 25% selecting "not sure/I don't make assumptions". With this, male sports identity is treated as inherently credible.

05

Assumptions of Female Sports Fans

The participants were asked: "Which of the following best describes your first assumption when a women identifies as a sports fan?" Both men and women still led with "she genuinely enjoys the sport" at 50%. Among women respondents, 25% assumed "she follows it casually" and 25% chose "not sure / I don't make assumptions." Men's responses were more distributed with fandom attributed to a partner or family member (16.7%) and "not sure" (16.7%) appearing alongside genuine enjoyment. With this, women's sports credibility is not treated as a default.

07 - Analysis

Overall Findings

Across all five questions, a consistent pattern emerges: women face a credibility gap in sports spaces that men simply do not experience. These findings from young adults ages 18–25 suggest that gender-based stereotyping in sports culture is not in the past, it is actively shaping the experiences of the younger generations.

The Comfort Divide

Women feel 5× less comfortable identifying as sports fans in front of men compared to other women. Men experience little variation across social contexts, pointing to a structural asymmetry in who gets to feel at home in sports culture.

The Credibility Gap

Not a single woman surveyed had escaped having her sports knowledge questioned because of her gender. Not a single man had experienced this. This is not a matter of perception — it is a documented lived reality.

The Assumption Asymmetry

Men are automatically assumed to be genuine fans. Women are more likely to be assumed to follow casually, or because of a partner. These assumptions exist even among women themselves, reflecting how deeply internalized these stereotypes are.

Confirming the Hypothesis

The data supports the working hypothesis: women are deterred from sports conversations and are not taken as seriously as men when they engage. Fear of being doubted or corrected is not irrational, it is a reasonable response to a real pattern.
